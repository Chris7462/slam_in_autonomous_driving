\section{Continuous-Time ESKF Error-State Derivation}

\subsection{Nominal state and IMU motion model}

Define the nominal state
\[
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
p & v & R & b_g & b_a & g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

The continuous-time IMU motion equations are
\begin{align}
\dot p &= v,\\
\dot v &= R(\tilde a-b_a-\eta_a)+g,\\
\dot R &= R(\tilde\omega-b_g-\eta_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_g &= \eta_{bg},\\
\dot b_a &= \eta_{ba},\\
\dot g &= 0.
\end{align}

The IMU measurement equations may be written as
\begin{align}
\tilde a &= R^T(a-g)+b_a+\eta_a,\\
\tilde\omega &= \omega+b_g+\eta_g,
\end{align}
together with
\[
\dot R=R\omega^\wedge.
\]

For the nominal state, the kinematic equations have the same form but
ignore the measurement-noise terms:
\begin{align}
\dot p &= v,\\
\dot v &= R(\tilde a-b_a)+g,\\
\dot R &= R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_g &= 0,\\
\dot b_a &= 0,\\
\dot g &= 0.
\end{align}

\subsection{True state}

Define the true state as
\[
x_t=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_t & v_t & R_t & b_{at} & b_{gt} & g_t
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

Its continuous-time dynamics are
\begin{align}
\dot p_t &= v_t,\\
\dot v_t &= R_t(\tilde a-b_{at}-\eta_a)+g_t,\\
\dot R_t &= R_t(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_{gt} &= \eta_{bg},\\
\dot b_{at} &= \eta_{ba},\\
\dot g_t &= 0.
\end{align}

\subsection{Definition of the error state}

Use a right-multiplicative rotation error:
\begin{align}
p_t &= p+\delta p,\\
v_t &= v+\delta v,\\
R_t &= R\,\delta R
    =R\Exp(\delta\theta),\\
b_{gt} &= b_g+\delta b_g,\\
b_{at} &= b_a+\delta b_a,\\
g_t &= g+\delta g.
\end{align}

The quantities without the subscript $t$ are nominal-state variables.
The goal is to derive the continuous-time dynamics of
\[
\delta x=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p &
\delta v &
\delta\theta &
\delta b_g &
\delta b_a &
\delta g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

\subsection{Position error}

Starting from
\[
p_t=p+\delta p,
\]
differentiate with respect to time:
\[
\dot p_t=\dot p+\delta\dot p.
\]

Since
\[
\dot p_t=v_t=v+\delta v
\]
and
\[
\dot p=v,
\]
we obtain
\[
v+\delta v=v+\delta\dot p.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot p=\delta v.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Velocity error}

Start from
\[
v_t=v+\delta v.
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot v_t=\dot v+\delta\dot v.
\]

Using the true-state equation,
\[
\dot v_t
=
R_t(\tilde a-b_{at}-\eta_a)+g_t.
\]

Substitute the error-state definitions:
\[
\dot v_t
=
R\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde a-b_a-\delta b_a-\eta_a)
+g+\delta g.
\]

For small $\delta\theta$,
\[
\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
I+\delta\theta^\wedge
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2).
\]

Keeping only first-order error terms,
\begin{align}
\dot v_t
&\approx
R(I+\delta\theta^\wedge)
(\tilde a-b_a-\delta b_a-\eta_a)
+g+\delta g\\
&\approx
R\tilde a-Rb_a-R\delta b_a-R\eta_a
+R\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde a
-R\delta\theta^\wedge b_a
+g+\delta g.
\end{align}

Terms such as
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge\delta b_a,
\qquad
\delta\theta^\wedge\eta_a
\]
are neglected as higher-order terms in this first-order error-state
linearization.

Using
\[
a^\wedge b=-b^\wedge a,
\]
we have
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde a
=
-\tilde a^\wedge\delta\theta,
\qquad
-\delta\theta^\wedge b_a
=
b_a^\wedge\delta\theta.
\]

Hence
\[
\dot v_t
\approx
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-R\eta_a
+\delta g.
\]

On the other hand, the nominal equation gives
\[
\dot v+\delta\dot v
=
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g+\delta\dot v.
\]

Comparing the two expressions,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g.
}
\end{equation}

Here we use the standard ESKF noise assumption adopted in the text:
multiplying the zero-mean white accelerometer noise by a rotation does
not change its distribution. Thus, in distribution,
\[
R\eta_a \sim \eta_a,
\]
and the rotated noise term is written simply as
\[
R\eta_a \;\Rightarrow\; \eta_a.
\]
This is a distributional simplification rather than a pointwise identity
between individual noise samples.

\subsection{Rotation error}

Start from
\[
R_t=R\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot R_t
=
\dot R\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]

\subsubsection*{Exact derivative of the exponential map}

For a general time-varying
\[
\delta\theta(t)\in\R^3,
\]
the exact derivative is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge,
}
\end{equation}
where $J_r(\delta\theta)$ is the right Jacobian of $\SO(3)$.

The formula above can be seen directly from the limit definition of the
right Jacobian.

Define
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

The right/local tangent velocity of $Q(t)$ is
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left(
Q(t)^{-1}Q(t+h)
\right)
}{h}.
\]

Since
\[
Q(t)^{-1}
=
\Exp(-\delta\theta(t))
\]
and
\[
Q(t+h)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+h)),
\]
we have
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\delta\theta(t))
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+h))
\right]
}{h}.
\]

Using the ordinary first-order expansion
\[
\delta\theta(t+h)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
h\,\delta\dot\theta(t)
+
O(h^2),
\]
this becomes
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
\delta\theta+h\,\delta\dot\theta
\right)
\right]
}{h}.
\]

Now compare this with the directional-limit definition of the right
Jacobian:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)u
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\phi)
\Exp(\phi+hu)
\right]
}{h}.
}
\end{equation}

By identifying
\[
\phi=\delta\theta,
\qquad
u=\delta\dot\theta,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Since a right/local tangent velocity satisfies
\[
\dot Q
=
Q\,\omega_{\mathrm{local}}^\wedge,
\]
we get
\[
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\]

Substituting
\[
Q=\Exp(\delta\theta)
\]
gives the exact relation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, this is the same relation as the compact right-Jacobian
increment formula
\[
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

To see why these two forms are equivalent, define
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

Starting from the differential form,
\[
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge,
\]
the ordinary first-order Taylor expansion in time gives
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)+\dot Q(t)\,dt+O(dt^2).
\]

Substituting the differential equation,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Factoring out $Q(t)$,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
\left[
I+
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
\right]
+
O(dt^2).
\]

For a small vector $\epsilon$,
\[
\Exp(\epsilon)
=
I+\epsilon^\wedge+O(\|\epsilon\|^2).
\]

Choose
\[
\epsilon
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt.
\]

Then
\[
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
=
I+
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Therefore
\[
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

Since
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t))
\]
and
\[
\delta\theta(t+dt)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
\delta\dot\theta(t)\,dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Conversely, start from the increment form:
\[
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

Using the small-angle expansion of the second exponential,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Hence
\[
\frac{Q(t+dt)-Q(t)}{dt}
=
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge
+
O(dt).
\]

Taking the limit $dt\to0$ gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Thus the increment form and the differential form describe the same
local motion:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
\\[4mm]
\Updownarrow\; dt\to0
\\[4mm]
\dfrac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

The upper expression is a first-order finite-increment relation, while
the lower expression is the corresponding exact instantaneous
differential relation.

Since
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)
=
I-\frac12\delta\theta^\wedge
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2),
\]
the first-order ESKF approximation is
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\approx
\delta\dot\theta.
\]

Therefore, within the first-order error-state derivation,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Using this first-order approximation,
\[
\dot R_t
\approx
\dot R\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
\]

The nominal rotation dynamics are
\[
\dot R
=
R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge.
\]

Hence
\[
\dot R_t
\approx
R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
\]

The true rotation dynamics are
\[
\dot R_t
=
R_t(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge
=
R\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge.
\]

Equating both expressions and canceling $R$ gives
\begin{align}
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge
&=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge\\
&\quad
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge
\Exp(\delta\theta).
\end{align}

Use the $\SO(3)$ identity
\[
\phi^\wedge R
=
R(R^T\phi)^\wedge.
\]

With
\[
R=\Exp(\delta\theta),
\]
this gives
\[
(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)
\right)^\wedge.
\]

Therefore
\begin{align}
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge
&=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Big[
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge\\
&\qquad
-\left(
\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)
\right)^\wedge
\Big].
\end{align}

For small $\delta\theta$,
\[
\Exp(-\delta\theta)
\approx
I-\delta\theta^\wedge.
\]

Thus
\begin{align}
&(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)
-\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)\\
&\approx
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)
-(I-\delta\theta^\wedge)(\tilde\omega-b_g)\\
&=
b_g-b_{gt}-\eta_g
+\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde\omega
-\delta\theta^\wedge b_g.
\end{align}

Since
\[
b_{gt}=b_g+\delta b_g,
\]
we have
\[
b_g-b_{gt}=-\delta b_g.
\]

Also,
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge(\tilde\omega-b_g)
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta.
\]

Therefore
\[
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g.
\]

Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Identity used above}

For $R\in\SO(3)$,
\[
\operatorname{Ad}_R\phi
=
(R\phi^\wedge R^T)^\vee
=
R\phi.
\]

Thus
\[
R\phi^\wedge R^T=(R\phi)^\wedge.
\]

From this,
\[
\phi^\wedge R
=
R(R^T\phi)^\wedge.
\]

\subsection{Supplement: From Finite Infinitesimal Increments to Instantaneous Velocity}

This appendix clarifies how the right-Jacobian finite-increment relation
is used to obtain the instantaneous right/local tangent velocity.

Let
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

For a small time increment $dt$,
\[
\delta\theta(t+dt)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
\delta\dot\theta(t)\,dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
and therefore
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+dt))
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt).
\]

The right-Jacobian increment relation is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Exp(\phi+\Delta\phi)
\approx
\Exp(\phi)
\Exp\!\left(J_r(\phi)\Delta\phi\right).
}
\end{equation}

Set
\[
\phi=\delta\theta,
\qquad
\Delta\phi=\delta\dot\theta\,dt.
\]

Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Now define the instantaneous right/local tangent velocity
$\omega_R$ by the infinitesimal motion
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)\Exp(\omega_R\,dt).
}
\end{equation}

Comparing the two infinitesimal expressions gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_R
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Thus $\delta\dot\theta$ is the time derivative of the exponential
coordinates, whereas $J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta$ is the
corresponding right/local tangent velocity of the rotation matrix.

To recover the ordinary matrix derivative, use
\[
\Exp(\omega_R\,dt)
=
I+\omega_R^\wedge dt+O(dt^2).
\]

Hence
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)\omega_R^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
so
\[
\frac{Q(t+dt)-Q(t)}{dt}
=
Q(t)\omega_R^\wedge
+
O(dt).
\]

Taking $dt\to0$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot Q
=
Q\omega_R^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Substituting
\[
\omega_R
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\]
and
\[
Q=\Exp(\delta\theta)
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Therefore the finite infinitesimal increment and the instantaneous
differential form are two descriptions of the same local motion:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
\\[4mm]
\Downarrow\quad dt\to0
\\[4mm]
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Exact and small-angle forms of the right Jacobian}

For $\phi\in\R^3$, let
\[
\vartheta=\|\phi\|.
\]

The exact right Jacobian of $\SO(3)$ is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)
=
I
-
\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\vartheta^2}\phi^\wedge
+
\frac{\vartheta-\sin\vartheta}{\vartheta^3}
(\phi^\wedge)^2.
}
\end{equation}

The coefficient of the first-order term follows from
\[
\cos\vartheta
=
1-\frac{\vartheta^2}{2!}
+\frac{\vartheta^4}{4!}
-\cdots,
\]
so
\[
\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\vartheta^2}
=
\frac12-\frac{\vartheta^2}{24}+\cdots.
\]

Similarly,
\[
\sin\vartheta
=
\vartheta-\frac{\vartheta^3}{3!}
+\frac{\vartheta^5}{5!}
-\cdots,
\]
which gives
\[
\frac{\vartheta-\sin\vartheta}{\vartheta^3}
=
\frac16-\frac{\vartheta^2}{120}+\cdots.
\]

Therefore
\[
J_r(\phi)
=
I
-\frac12\phi^\wedge
+\frac16(\phi^\wedge)^2
+O(\|\phi\|^3),
\]
and, retaining only first-order terms,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)
=
I-\frac12\phi^\wedge+O(\|\phi\|^2).
}
\end{equation}

For the ESKF rotation error, $\phi=\delta\theta$ is small. Hence
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
=
\delta\dot\theta
-\frac12
\delta\theta^\wedge\delta\dot\theta
+\cdots.
\]

Under the first-order error-state approximation, the product involving
$\delta\theta$ and $\delta\dot\theta$ is neglected, yielding
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\approx
\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

This is why the ESKF derivation commonly uses
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge,
\]
even though the exact differential relation contains the right
Jacobian.

\subsection{Gyroscope-bias error}

Starting from
\[
b_{gt}=b_g+\delta b_g,
\]
differentiate:
\[
\dot b_{gt}
=
\dot b_g+\delta\dot b_g.
\]

The true bias dynamics are
\[
\dot b_{gt}=\eta_{bg},
\]
while the nominal model ignores noise:
\[
\dot b_g=0.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Accelerometer-bias error}

Similarly,
\[
b_{at}=b_a+\delta b_a.
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot b_{at}
=
\dot b_a+\delta\dot b_a.
\]

Using
\[
\dot b_{at}=\eta_{ba},
\qquad
\dot b_a=0,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gravity error}

Starting from
\[
g_t=g+\delta g,
\]
differentiate:
\[
\dot g_t=\dot g+\delta\dot g.
\]

Since
\[
\dot g_t=0,
\qquad
\dot g=0,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot g=0.
}
\end{equation}

\section{Final continuous-time error-state equations}

Collecting the results,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta\dot p
&=
\delta v,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot v
&=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot\theta
&=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot b_g
&=
\eta_{bg},\\[1mm]
\delta\dot b_a
&=
\eta_{ba},\\[1mm]
\delta\dot g
&=
0.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}



\section{Discrete-Time ESKF Kinematic Equation}

\subsection*{Notation convention}

In the continuous-time derivations below, $t$, $\tau$, and $s$ denote
physical time. After discretization, the final propagation equations
use the discrete time-step indices $k$ and $k+1$, with
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
t_k=t,\qquad t_{k+1}=t_k+\Delta t=t+\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

Thus, continuous-time integration uses $t,\tau,s$, while final
discrete-time propagation equations use $k,k+1$.


\subsection{Nominal-state position equation}

For the nominal state,
\[
\dot p=v,
\qquad
\dot v=R(\tilde a-b_a)+g.
\]

Define the acceleration expressed in the world frame as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
a_W(t)=R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g.
}
\end{equation}

Since
\[
\dot p(\tau)=v(\tau),
\]
integrating from $t$ to $t+\Delta t$ gives
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+\int_t^{t+\Delta t}v(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

The velocity at an intermediate time $\tau$ is
\[
v(\tau)
=
v(t)+\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds.
\]

Substituting this into the position integral,
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\left[
v(t)+\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds
\right]d\tau.
\]

\subsubsection*{Assumption over one IMU sampling interval}

To obtain the discrete-time equation used here, assume that the
world-frame acceleration is approximately constant over the short IMU
sampling interval:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
a_W(s)\approx a_W(t),
\qquad
s\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, over this interval we use the values at the beginning of
the interval,
\[
R(s)\approx R(t),
\qquad
\tilde a(s)\approx\tilde a(t),
\qquad
b_a(s)\approx b_a(t),
\]
with gravity $g$ treated as constant. Thus
\[
a_W(s)
\approx
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g.
\]

Under this approximation,
\[
\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds
\approx
a_W(t)(\tau-t).
\]

Therefore
\begin{align}
p(t+\Delta t)
&=
p(t)+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\left[
v(t)+a_W(t)(\tau-t)
\right]d\tau\\
&=
p(t)
+
v(t)\Delta t
+
a_W(t)
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}(\tau-t)\,d\tau.
\end{align}

The remaining integral is
\[
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}(\tau-t)\,d\tau
=
\frac12\Delta t^2.
\]

Hence
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+v(t)\Delta t
+\frac12a_W(t)\Delta t^2.
\]

Substituting the expression for $a_W(t)$,
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+v(t)\Delta t
+
\frac12
\left[
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g
\right]
\Delta t^2.
\]

Finally,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
p_{k+1}
=
p_k+v_k\Delta t
+
\frac12R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})\Delta t^2
+
\frac12g_k\Delta t^2.
}
\end{equation}

This is the discrete-time nominal position propagation equation.  The
appearance of $R(t)$ rather than $R(t+\Delta t)$ follows from the
beginning-of-interval approximation above.  In the exact continuous
motion, $R(s)$ and therefore $a_W(s)$ generally vary throughout the
sampling interval.

\subsection{Nominal-state velocity equation}

Starting from
\[
\dot v
=
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g
\equiv
a_W,
\]
integrate over the interval $[t,t+\Delta t]$:
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
=
v(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
a_W(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

Using the same assumption as in the position derivation,
\[
a_W(\tau)\approx a_W(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t],
\]
we obtain
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
\approx
v(t)
+
a_W(t)
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}d\tau.
\]

Therefore
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
=
v(t)
+
a_W(t)\Delta t.
\]

Substituting
\[
a_W(t)
=
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g,
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
v_{k+1}
=
v_k
+
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})\Delta t
+
g_k\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

This is the discrete-time nominal velocity propagation equation.  As
with the position equation, the approximation uses the beginning-of-
interval values $R(t)$, $\tilde a(t)$, and $b_a(t)$ over one IMU
sampling interval.


\subsection{Nominal-state rotation equation}

The continuous-time nominal rotation dynamics are
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot R(t)
=
R(t)(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Define the bias-corrected body-frame angular velocity
\[
\omega_B(t)
\equiv
\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t).
\]

Over one short IMU sampling interval, assume
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_B(\tau)\approx\omega_B(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently,
\[
\tilde\omega(\tau)\approx\tilde\omega(t),
\qquad
b_g(\tau)\approx b_g(t)
\]
over this interval.  The differential equation therefore becomes
\[
\dot R(\tau)
=
R(\tau)\omega_B(t)^\wedge,
\]
where $\omega_B(t)^\wedge$ is constant with respect to $\tau$.

The solution of this matrix differential equation with initial
condition $R(t)$ is
\[
R(\tau)
=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right),
\]
where $\exp(\cdot)$ denotes the matrix exponential.

This can be verified directly:
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{d\tau}R(\tau)
&=
R(t)
\frac{d}{d\tau}
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right)
\\
&=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right)
\omega_B(t)^\wedge
\\
&=
R(\tau)\omega_B(t)^\wedge.
\end{align}

At $\tau=t+\Delta t$,
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge\Delta t
\right).
\]

Since
\[
\Exp(\phi)
\equiv
\exp(\phi^\wedge)
\]
and
\[
(\omega_B\Delta t)^\wedge
=
\omega_B^\wedge\Delta t,
\]
we obtain
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
R(t)
\Exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)\Delta t
\right).
\]

Substituting
\[
\omega_B(t)
=
\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t),
\]
gives the discrete-time nominal rotation propagation equation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_{k+1}
=
R_k
\Exp\!\left(
(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right).
}
\end{equation}

The exponential increment appears on the right because
\[
\dot R=R\omega_B^\wedge
\]
uses the body-frame (right-trivialized) angular velocity.  In contrast,
if the angular velocity were expressed in the world frame so that
\[
\dot R=\omega_W^\wedge R,
\]
the corresponding constant-angular-velocity propagation would be
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
\Exp(\omega_W\Delta t)R(t).
\]



\subsection{Nominal-state bias and gravity equations}

For the nominal state, the gyroscope bias, accelerometer bias, and
gravity are modeled as constant during propagation:
\[
\dot b_g=0,
\qquad
\dot b_a=0,
\qquad
\dot g=0.
\]

Integrating the gyroscope-bias equation over one sampling interval,
\[
b_g(t+\Delta t)
=
b_g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot b_g(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
b_{g,k+1}=b_{g,k}.
}
\end{equation}

Similarly,
\[
b_a(t+\Delta t)
=
b_a(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot b_a(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
so
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
b_{a,k+1}=b_{a,k}.
}
\end{equation}

For gravity,
\[
g(t+\Delta t)
=
g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot g(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
g_{k+1}=g_k.
}
\end{equation}

The constant nominal-bias equations do not imply that the physical IMU
biases are assumed to remain exactly constant.  Their random-walk
variations are represented in the error-state dynamics by
\[
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg},
\qquad
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba},
\]
and their uncertainty is propagated through the error-state covariance.


\subsection{Summary of the discrete-time nominal-state equations}

Collecting the nominal-state propagation equations,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p_{k+1}
&=
p_k+v_k\Delta t
+\frac12
\left[
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})+g_k
\right]\Delta t^2,
\\[1mm]
v_{k+1}
&=
v_k+
\left[
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})+g_k
\right]\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
R_{k+1}
&=
R_k
\Exp\!\left(
(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right),
\\[1mm]
b_{g,k+1}
&=
b_{g,k},
\\
b_{a,k+1}
&=
b_{a,k},
\\
g_{k+1}
&=
g_k.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-time error-state equations}

We now discretize the continuous-time error-state equations derived
previously.

\subsubsection*{Position error}

From the continuous-time position-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot p=\delta v,
\]
assume that $\delta v$ is approximately constant over one short
sampling interval $[t,t+\Delta t]$. Then
\begin{align}
\delta p(t+\Delta t)-\delta p(t)
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta\dot p(\tau)\,d\tau\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta v(\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\approx
\delta v(t)\Delta t.
\end{align}

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta p_{k+1}
=
\delta p_k+\delta v_k\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Velocity error}

From the continuous-time velocity-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g,
\]
define the deterministic part
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
f_v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
+\delta g.
}
\end{equation}

Then
\[
\delta\dot v=f_v-\eta_a.
\]

Integrating over one sampling interval,
\begin{align}
\delta v(t+\Delta t)-\delta v(t)
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f_v(\tau)\,d\tau
-
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_a(\tau)\,d\tau.
\end{align}

Assume that the deterministic quantities in $f_v$ are approximately
constant over the short interval:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
f_v(\tau)\approx f_v(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Thus
\[
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f_v(\tau)\,d\tau
\approx
f_v(t)\Delta t.
\]

Define the integrated accelerometer-noise increment
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_v
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_a(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

Assume the continuous-time accelerometer noise is zero-mean white
Gaussian noise with
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_a(t)]=0
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_a(t)\eta_a(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_a^2 I\,\delta(t-s).
}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\sigma_a^2$ is the continuous-time white-noise intensity
(per axis).  Then the integrated noise increment also has zero mean:
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_v]=0.
\]

Its covariance is
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_v)
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_v\eta_v^T
\right]
\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_a(\tau)\eta_a(s)^T
\right]
\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\sigma_a^2 I
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\delta(\tau-s)\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\boxed{
\sigma_a^2\Delta t\,I.
}
\end{align}

Thus, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_v)
=
\sigma_a^2\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

The book distinguishes the sampled accelerometer measurement noise from
the continuous-time noise-density parameter through
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma_a(k)
=
\frac{\sigma_a}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
}
\end{equation}

Therefore the standard deviation of the integrated velocity-noise
increment can be written in either equivalent form:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma(\eta_v)
=
\Delta t\,\sigma_a(k)
=
\sigma_a\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

The first expression uses the sampled-noise notation, while the
second follows directly after substituting the sampled-noise relation.

Note that writing simply
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_a)=\sigma_a^2
\]
for continuous-time white noise is shorthand for its noise intensity.
The Dirac-delta covariance above makes the continuous-to-discrete
scaling explicit.

Hence
\[
\delta v(t+\Delta t)
\approx
\delta v(t)+f_v(t)\Delta t-\eta_v.
\]

Substituting the expression for $f_v(t)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta v(t+\Delta t)
&=
\delta v(t)
-R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))^\wedge
 \delta\theta(t)\Delta t\\
&\quad
-R(t)\delta b_a(t)\Delta t
+\delta g(t)\Delta t
-\eta_v.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

The term $\eta_v$ is the discrete-time noise increment obtained by
integrating the continuous-time accelerometer noise over the sampling
interval; unlike the deterministic term $f_v(t)\Delta t$, it should not
be interpreted simply as a deterministic constant multiplied by
$\Delta t$.


\subsubsection*{Rotation error}

From the continuous-time rotation-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g,
\]
define
\[
A
\equiv
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge.
\]

Over one short IMU sampling interval, assume that
\[
\tilde\omega(\tau)\approx\tilde\omega(t),
\qquad
b_g(\tau)\approx b_g(t),
\]
so that $A$ is approximately constant.  The homogeneous equation
\[
\dot x=Ax
\]
has the solution
\[
x(t+\Delta t)=\exp(A\Delta t)x(t).
\]

Therefore the homogeneous part of the rotation error propagates as
\[
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
=
\exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))^\wedge\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta(t).
\]

Using the $\SO(3)$ exponential-map notation,
\[
\exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Delta t
\right)
=
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)\Delta t
\right).
\]

For the forcing terms $-\delta b_g-\eta_g$, the first-order
discretization used here gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
&\approx
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta(t)
\\
&\quad
-\delta b_g(t)\Delta t
-\eta_\theta,
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
where the integrated gyroscope-noise increment is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_\theta
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_g(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

More exactly, for constant
\[
A=-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge,
\]
the variation-of-constants formula is
\[
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
=
e^{A\Delta t}\delta\theta(t)
-
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
e^{A(t+\Delta t-\tau)}
\left[
\delta b_g(\tau)+\eta_g(\tau)
\right]d\tau.
\]
The expression above with
$-\delta b_g\Delta t-\eta_\theta$ is the corresponding first-order
forcing-term approximation.

\paragraph{Variance of the discrete rotation-noise increment.}

Assume the continuous-time gyroscope noise is zero-mean isotropic white
Gaussian noise with
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_g(t)]=0
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_g(t)\eta_g(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_g^2 I\,\delta(t-s).
}
\end{equation}

Then
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_\theta]=0
\]
and
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_\theta)
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_\theta\eta_\theta^T
\right]
\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_g(\tau)\eta_g(s)^T
\right]
\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\boxed{
\sigma_g^2\Delta t\,I.
}
\end{align}

Thus, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_\theta)
=
\sigma_g^2\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

For the sampled gyroscope measurement noise, the book uses
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma_g(k)
=
\frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
}
\end{equation}

Hence the standard deviation of the integrated rotation-noise increment
can be written as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma(\eta_\theta)
=
\Delta t\,\sigma_g(k)
=
\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

Again, the first form uses the sampled-noise notation, while the second is the
equivalent expression in terms of the continuous-time noise-density
parameter.  The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ factor belongs to the standard
deviation, not to the variance.  The same covariance result also holds if the
exact state-transition rotation inside the noise integral is retained:
because $A$ is skew-symmetric, $e^{As}$ is a rotation matrix, and for
isotropic noise
\[
e^{As}(\sigma_g^2 I)e^{As\,T}
=
\sigma_g^2 I.
\]

\subsubsection*{Gyroscope-bias error}

Here we explicitly distinguish the continuous-time bias random-walk
process from its integrated discrete-time increment.  The continuous
process is denoted by
\[
\eta_{bg}(t),
\]
while
\[
\eta_{bg,d}
\]
denotes the noise accumulated over one sampling interval.  The book
reuses the symbol $\eta_{bg}$ for the discrete increment in
Eqs.~(3.42)--(3.43); the subscript $d$ is retained here to avoid
ambiguity.

From
\[
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg},
\]
integrating over one sampling interval gives
\[
\delta b_g(t+\Delta t)
=
\delta b_g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{bg}(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

Define
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_{bg,d}
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{bg}(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta b_{g,k+1}
=
\delta b_{g,k}+\eta_{bg,d}.
}
\end{equation}

If
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_{bg}(t)\eta_{bg}(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_{bg}^2 I\,\delta(t-s),
\]
then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{bg,d})
=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t\,I,
}
\end{equation}
or, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{bg,d})
=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t,
\qquad
\sigma_{\eta_{bg,d}}
=
\sigma_{bg}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Accelerometer-bias error}

Likewise, $\eta_{ba}(t)$ denotes the continuous-time accelerometer-bias
random-walk process, while $\eta_{ba,d}$ denotes its integrated
discrete-time increment over one sampling interval.

Similarly, from
\[
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba},
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta b_a(t+\Delta t)
=
\delta b_a(t)+\eta_{ba,d},
}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_{ba,d}
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{ba}(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

For continuous-time isotropic white noise intensity
$\sigma_{ba}^2$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{ba,d})
=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t\,I,
}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{ba,d})
=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t,
\qquad
\sigma_{\eta_{ba,d}}
=
\sigma_{ba}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Gravity error}

From
\[
\delta\dot g=0,
\]
integration gives
\[
\delta g(t+\Delta t)-\delta g(t)
=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta\dot g(\tau)\,d\tau
=
0.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta g_{k+1}
=
\delta g_k.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Summary of the discrete-time error-state equations}

Collecting the discrete-time error-state propagation equations,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta p_{k+1}
&=
\delta p_k+\delta v_k\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\delta v_{k+1}
&=
\delta v_k
-R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})^\wedge\delta\theta_k\Delta t
-R_k\delta b_{a,k}\Delta t
+\delta g_k\Delta t
-\eta_v,
\\[1mm]
\delta\theta_{k+1}
&\approx
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta_k
-\delta b_{g,k}\Delta t
-\eta_\theta,
\\[1mm]
\delta b_{g,k+1}
&=
\delta b_{g,k}+\eta_{bg,d},
\\
\delta b_{a,k+1}
&=
\delta b_{a,k}+\eta_{ba,d},
\\
\delta g_{k+1}
&=
\delta g_k.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

It is useful to list both the standard deviations and variances.  The sampled IMU measurement-noise
standard deviations satisfy
\[
\sigma_a(k)=\frac{\sigma_a}{\sqrt{\Delta t}},
\qquad
\sigma_g(k)=\frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
\]

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\eta_v)
&=
\Delta t\,\sigma_a(k)
=
\sigma_a\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_v)
&=
\sigma_a^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_\theta)
&=
\Delta t\,\sigma_g(k)
=
\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_\theta)
&=
\sigma_g^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_{bg,d})
&=
\sigma_{bg}\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{bg,d})
&=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_{ba,d})
&=
\sigma_{ba}\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{ba,d})
&=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

The first two standard-deviation expressions use the sampled IMU-noise
notation.  For the bias random walks, the book denotes the
integrated discrete noises again by $\eta_{bg}$ and $\eta_{ba}$; this
note uses $\eta_{bg,d}$ and $\eta_{ba,d}$ so that they remain distinct
from the continuous-time processes $\eta_{bg}(t)$ and $\eta_{ba}(t)$.


\section{Motion Process of ESKF}

Using the discrete-time error-state equations derived above, write the
error-state motion model as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x_{k+1}
=
f(\delta x_k)+w_k,
\qquad
w_k\sim\mathcal N(0,Q_k).
}
\end{equation}

The discrete process-noise covariance is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q_k
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
0_3,\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_v),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_\theta),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{bg,d}),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{ba,d}),\,
0_3
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Here the subscript $d$ is retained for the bias-noise increments in
order to distinguish them from the corresponding continuous-time
white-noise processes.

\subsection{Linearized form}

Linearize the discrete transition function $f$ about the nominal
error-state mean.  For a general linearization point
$\bar{\delta x}_k$,
\[
f(\delta x_k)
\approx
f(\bar{\delta x}_k)
+
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=\bar{\delta x}_k}
(\delta x_k-\bar{\delta x}_k).
\]

In the ESKF, the error-state mean is reset to zero, so the
linearization point is
\[
\bar{\delta x}_k=0.
\]

Therefore,
\[
f(\delta x_k)
\approx
f(0)
+
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=0}
\delta x_k.
\]

For the error-state motion model,
\[
f(0)=0.
\]

Define the discrete-time error-state transition Jacobian as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
F_k
\triangleq
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=0}.
}
\end{equation}

The linearized error-state propagation is therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x_{k+1}
\approx
F_k\delta x_k+w_k.
}
\end{equation}

Thus, $F_k$ is the Jacobian of the \emph{discrete-time error-state
transition function} with respect to the error state, evaluated at
zero error.  It should not be confused with the time derivative
$d\delta x/dt$.

\subsection{State transition Jacobian matrix}

The error-state ordering is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p &
\delta v &
\delta\theta &
\delta b_g &
\delta b_a &
\delta g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
}
\end{equation}

Since the discrete-time error-state equations have already been
linearized explicitly, the transition Jacobian can be read directly
from the coefficients multiplying each error-state component.  With
the ordering above,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
F_k
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3
&
I_3\Delta t
&
0
&
0
&
0
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&
I_3
&
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\Delta t
&
0
&
-R\Delta t
&
I_3\Delta t
\\[1mm]
0
&
0
&
\Exp\!\left(-(\tilde\omega-b_g)\Delta t\right)
&
-I_3\Delta t
&
0
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&
0
&
0
&
I_3
&
0
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&
0
&
0
&
0
&
I_3
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&

&
0
&

&

&
I_3
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

Each entry is a $3\times3$ block, so $F_k\in\R^{18\times18}$.

\subsection{ESKF IMU propagation flow}

For each new IMU measurement, the ESKF prediction process can be
summarized as follows:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{New IMU measurement}
\\
(\tilde a_k,\ \tilde\omega_k,\ \Delta t)
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Nominal-state prediction}
\\
\text{using the discrete-time nominal-state equations}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
[p,\ v,\ R,\ b_g,\ b_a,\ g]^T
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Build the error-state transition matrix }F_k
\\
\text{using the error-state transition Jacobian}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[-1mm]
\begin{array}{ccc}
\delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_k\delta x_k
&
\qquad
&
P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_kP_kF_k^T+Q_k
\\
\text{usually remains zero}
&&
\text{propagate uncertainty}
\end{array}
\\[3mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Predicted ESKF state}
\\
\left(
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}},
\ \delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}},
\ P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
\right).
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

Mathematically, the prediction contains three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item propagate the nominal state using the IMU measurements;
    \item propagate the error-state mean,
    \[
    \delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}=F_k\delta x_k;
    \]
    \item propagate the error-state covariance,
    \[
    P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
    =
    F_kP_kF_k^T+Q_k.
    \]
\end{enumerate}

In the ESKF, the error-state mean is reset to zero after the update
step.  Therefore, in the usual prediction cycle,
\[
\delta x_k=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_k\delta x_k
=
0.
\]
Consequently, the explicit error-state mean propagation is often
unnecessary in an implementation.  The important quantities propagated
during the IMU prediction are the nominal state and the error-state
covariance.


\section{Update Process of ESKF}

\subsection{ESKF update flow}

The ESKF update process can be summarized as
\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{New sensor observation }z
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Construct the observation model}
\\
z=h(x)+v,\qquad v\sim\mathcal N(0,V)
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Compute the observation Jacobian }H
\\
H=\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial\delta x}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Compute the innovation}
\\
r=z-h(x_{\mathrm{pred}})
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Compute the Kalman gain}
\\
K=P_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T
\left(
HP_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T+V
\right)^{-1}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Estimate the error state}
\\
\delta x=Kr
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Update the error-state covariance}
\\
P=(I-KH)P_{\mathrm{pred}}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Pass }(\delta x,P)\text{ to ESKF post-processing}
\end{array}
}
\]

Thus, the observation update itself estimates the posterior error state
and its covariance.  The estimated error state is not yet the final
nominal-state correction at this stage.  The following post-processing
stage injects $\delta x$ into the nominal state, resets the error-state
mean to zero, and, when retained, transforms the covariance to the new
tangent-space reference.

For the full-pose GNSS implementation discussed below, the innovation
takes the manifold-aware form
\[
r_{\mathrm{GNSS}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{\mathrm{gnss}}-p\\
\Log(R^TR_{\mathrm{gnss}})
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and the direct error-state observation gives
\[
H_{\mathrm{GNSS}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&I_3&0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]


\subsection{Observation model}

Consider the general observation equation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
z=h(x)+v,
\qquad
v\sim\mathcal N(0,V).
}
\end{equation}

Here $z$ is the observation data, $v$ is the observation noise, and
$V$ is the observation-noise covariance matrix.

In a traditional EKF, the observation equation is linearized with
respect to the state variables. In the ESKF, the Kalman-filter update
is performed with respect to the error-state variables. Therefore, the
required observation Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H
=
\left.
\frac{\partial h}{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{x_{\mathrm{pred}}}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kalman update equations}

Once the observation Jacobian is available, the Kalman gain is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
K
=
P_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T
\left(
HP_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T+V
\right)^{-1}.
}
\end{equation}

The error-state update is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x
=
K\left(
z-h(x_{\mathrm{pred}})
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Following the notation used in this chapter, the nominal state is then
updated as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
x=x_{\mathrm{pred}}+\delta x.
}
\end{equation}

The covariance update is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P=(I-KH)P_{\mathrm{pred}}.
}
\end{equation}

Here $K$ is the Kalman gain, $P_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is the predicted
error-state covariance, and $P$ is the updated covariance.

\subsection{Observation Jacobian with respect to the error state}

For observations of the nominal state, the Jacobian with respect to the
error state can be obtained using the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H
=
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}.
}
\end{equation}

The chain of mappings is
\[
\boxed{
\delta x
\longrightarrow
x(\delta x)
\longrightarrow
h\!\left(x(\delta x)\right).
}
\]

Here $x(\delta x)$ denotes a local perturbation of the nominal state
used for linearization. It should not be confused with the true state.

\subsection{Local perturbation parameterization}

For the nominal-state ordering
\[
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
p&
v&
R&
b_g&
b_a&
g
\end{bmatrix}^{T},
\]
define the locally perturbed state by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
x(\delta x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p+\delta p\\
v+\delta v\\
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\\
b_g+\delta b_g\\
b_a+\delta b_a\\
g+\delta g
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
x(0)=x_{\mathrm{pred}}.
}
\end{equation}

For the Euclidean components,
\[
\frac{\partial(p+\delta p)}{\partial\delta p}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(v+\delta v)}{\partial\delta v}=I_3,
\]
and similarly
\[
\frac{\partial(b_g+\delta b_g)}{\partial\delta b_g}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(b_a+\delta b_a)}{\partial\delta b_a}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(g+\delta g)}{\partial\delta g}=I_3.
\]

The rotation component requires special treatment. Since the
perturbation is applied on the right,
\[
R(\delta\theta)=R\Exp(\delta\theta),
\]
and the corresponding rotation coordinate is
\[
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right).
\]

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\left.
\frac{
\partial
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right)
}{
\partial\delta\theta
}
\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
=
J_r^{-1}(\Log R).
\end{equation}

Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
J_r^{-1}(\Log R),\,
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
I_3
\right).
}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection*{Rotation block: chain-rule derivation}

The rotation block can also be derived by explicitly applying the
chain rule to the composition
\[
\delta\theta
\longrightarrow
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\longrightarrow
R\Exp(\delta\theta)
\longrightarrow
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right).
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}{\partial\delta\theta}
=
J^{\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}_{R\Exp(\delta\theta)}
\,
J^{R\Exp(\delta\theta)}_{\Exp(\delta\theta)}
\,
J^{\Exp(\delta\theta)}_{\delta\theta}.
\end{equation}

Using the right-trivialized Jacobians,
\[
J^{\Log(Q)}_{Q}=J_r^{-1}(\Log Q),
\qquad
J^{R Q}_{Q}=I_3,
\qquad
J^{\Exp(\delta\theta)}_{\delta\theta}=J_r(\delta\theta),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}{\partial\delta\theta}
=
J_r^{-1}\!\left(\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))\right)
J_r(\delta\theta).
\end{equation}

Evaluating at $\delta\theta=0$ and using
\[
\Exp(0)=I_3,
\qquad
J_r(0)=I_3,
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\left.
\frac{\partial\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}{\partial\delta\theta}
\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
=
J_r^{-1}(\Log R).
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Rotation block: BCH result}

Let
\[
\phi=\Log R,
\qquad
R=\Exp(\phi).
\]
Using the textbook right-BCH linear approximation,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Log\!\left(\Exp(\phi)\Exp(\delta\theta)\right)
\approx
\phi+J_r^{-1}(\phi)\delta\theta.
}
\end{equation}
Since $R=\Exp(\phi)$ and $\phi=\Log R$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\left.
\frac{\partial\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}
{\partial\delta\theta}
\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
=
J_r^{-1}(\Log R).
}
\end{equation}

The full BCH-series derivation is collected in the appendix.


\subsection{Full-pose GNSS observation}

For the full-pose GNSS case, the observation contains position and
rotation. With the nominal-state ordering above, the observation
function is conceptually
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
h(x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\
R
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

Thus the GNSS observation directly depends on the nominal position
$p$ and nominal rotation $R$, while it does not directly observe
$v$, $b_g$, $b_a$, or $g$.

The next step is to evaluate
\[
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial p}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial v}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial R}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial b_g}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial b_a}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial g}
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and then combine it with
$\partial x/\partial\delta x$. The treatment of the rotation
observation and its relation to the direct error-state GNSS
formulation is left for the following discussion.



\subsubsection*{Two observation formulations}

For the full-pose GNSS update, the position observation is
straightforward.  The rotation observation, however, can be formulated
in two different ways.

\subsubsection*{General chain-rule formulation}

If the GNSS rotation is represented in Lie-algebra coordinates, define
\[
\phi=\Log R,
\qquad
\phi_{\mathrm{gnss}}=\Log R_{\mathrm{gnss}}.
\]
The nominal-state observation function can then be written as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
h(x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\
\Log R
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

With the nominal-state ordering
\[
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
p&v&R&b_g&b_a&g
\end{bmatrix}^{T},
\]
the observation Jacobian with respect to the nominal-state coordinates
selects the position and rotation components:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&I_3&0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

From the local perturbation derivation above,
\[
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
J_r^{-1}(\Log R),\,
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
I_3
\right).
\]
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H_{\mathrm{chain}}
=
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&J_r^{-1}(\Log R)&0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

In this formulation, the rotation innovation is represented in
Lie-algebra coordinates as
\[
\Log R_{\mathrm{gnss}}-\Log R.
\]

\subsubsection*{Direct error-state observation for GNSS rotation}

For the GNSS implementation, a more natural formulation is available
because the ESKF rotation error is defined by right multiplication:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_{\mathrm{gnss}}
=
R\Exp(\delta\theta).
}
\end{equation}

Left-multiplying by $R^T$ gives
\[
R^TR_{\mathrm{gnss}}
=
\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]
Taking the logarithm yields
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
z_{\delta\theta}
=
\Log(R^TR_{\mathrm{gnss}})
=
\delta\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Thus the GNSS rotation is a direct observation of the rotation error
state, and its Jacobian is simply
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{\partial z_{\delta\theta}}
{\partial\delta\theta}
=
I_3.
}
\end{equation}

The position observation is similarly
\[
p_{\mathrm{gnss}}
=
p+\delta p,
\]
so
\[
\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{gnss}}}{\partial\delta p}
=
I_3.
\]

Consequently, the full-pose GNSS observation Jacobian used in the
implementation is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H_{\mathrm{GNSS}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3&0&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&I_3&0&0&0
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

The corresponding innovation is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
z-h(x_{\mathrm{pred}})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{\mathrm{gnss}}-p\\
\Log(R^TR_{\mathrm{gnss}})
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Which formulation is used?}

The general chain-rule formulation is mathematically valid when the
rotation observation is represented using the coordinate
$\Log R$.  In that case, the rotation Jacobian contains
$J_r^{-1}(\Log R)$.

For the full-pose GNSS update, however, the direct error-state
formulation is preferable because
\[
\Log(R^TR_{\mathrm{gnss}})
\]
is the relative rotation from the nominal estimate to the GNSS
observation and is exactly aligned with the right rotation error
$\delta\theta$.  Therefore the implementation uses
\[
\boxed{
H_R=I_3
}
\]
rather than approximating $J_r^{-1}(\Log R)$ by the identity.  The two
forms correspond to different observation representations; the
identity block is not obtained by discarding the right-Jacobian term.


\subsection{Body-frame 3D Wheel Velocity Observation}

\subsubsection*{Observation model}

The wheel encoder directly measures the forward body-frame speed. To form a
three-dimensional body-frame observation, we additionally impose approximate
zero lateral and vertical body velocities as vehicle-motion constraints:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
 z_b=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\mathrm{wheel}}&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{T},
 \qquad z_b=h(x)+n_b,\qquad n_b\sim\mathcal N(0,V_b).
}
\end{equation}
The last two components are pseudo-measurements, not independent readings
from the wheel encoder. With $R$ mapping body coordinates into world
coordinates and $v$ denoting world-frame velocity, the observation function is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{h(x)=R^Tv.}
\end{equation}
The ESKF error state is ordered as
$\delta x=[\delta p^T,\delta v^T,\delta\theta^T,
\delta b_g^T,\delta b_a^T,\delta g^T]^T$.

\subsubsection*{Velocity Jacobian}

With $v(\delta v)=v+\delta v$, keeping $R$ fixed,
\[
 h(\delta v)=R^T(v+\delta v)=R^Tv+R^T\delta v.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial\delta v}\right|_{\delta v=0}=R^T.}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Rotation Jacobian: Method 1 --- first-order expansion}

Using the right perturbation $R(\delta\theta)=R\Exp(\delta\theta)$,
\[
 h(\delta x)
 =\bigl(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\bigr)^T(v+\delta v)
 =\Exp(-\delta\theta)R^T(v+\delta v).
\]
The first-order exponential approximation is
$\Exp(-\delta\theta)\approx I-\delta\theta^\wedge$.
Neglecting the second-order product $\delta\theta^\wedge R^T\delta v$,
\begin{align}
 h(\delta x)
 &\approx (I-\delta\theta^\wedge)(R^Tv+R^T\delta v)\\
 &\approx R^Tv+R^T\delta v-\delta\theta^\wedge R^Tv\\
 &=R^Tv+R^T\delta v+(R^Tv)^\wedge\delta\theta.
\end{align}
We used $-a^\wedge b=b^\wedge a$. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial\delta\theta}\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
 =(R^Tv)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Rotation Jacobian: Method 2 --- directly from the limit definition}

Fix the nominal $R,v$ and let $u\in\mathbb R^3$ be an arbitrary
direction. The directional derivative at zero perturbation is
\begin{align}
 Dh(0)[u]
 &=\lim_{\epsilon\to0}
 \frac{\Exp(-\epsilon u)R^Tv-R^Tv}{\epsilon}\\
 &=\left(\lim_{\epsilon\to0}
 \frac{\Exp(-\epsilon u)-I}{\epsilon}\right)R^Tv\\
 &=-u^\wedge R^Tv\\
 &=(R^Tv)^\wedge u.
\end{align}
The limit in the second line equals $-u^\wedge$ by the definition
of the differential of the exponential map at the identity.
Because $u$ is arbitrary, the linear map is represented by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial\delta\theta}\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
 =(R^Tv)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}
Both methods therefore give the same sign and the same rotation block.

\subsubsection*{Complete error-state observation Jacobian}

The observation has no direct dependence on position, IMU biases, or gravity.
Collecting the six $3\times3$ blocks gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H_b=\begin{bmatrix}
0_{3\times3}&R^T&(R^Tv)^\wedge&0_{3\times3}&0_{3\times3}&0_{3\times3}
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{3\times18}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Measurement covariance and Kalman update}

We distinguish the uncertainty of the measured longitudinal wheel speed
from the uncertainty of the lateral and vertical motion constraints:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{V_b=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_x^2,\sigma_y^2,\sigma_z^2).}
\end{equation}
The values $\sigma_y^2$ and $\sigma_z^2$ express confidence in the
pseudo-measurements; they should be relaxed when sideslip, uneven ground,
or other violations of the zero-velocity assumptions are plausible.

The innovation and Kalman update are
\begin{align}
 r_b&=z_b-R^Tv,\\
 K&=P_{\mathrm{pred}}H_b^T
 (H_bP_{\mathrm{pred}}H_b^T+V_b)^{-1},\\
 \delta x&=Kr_b,\\
 P&=(I-KH_b)P_{\mathrm{pred}}.
\end{align}
The estimated correction and covariance are then passed to the
post-processing stage for nominal-state injection and reset.

\subsubsection*{Comparison with the textbook's world-frame simplification}

The textbook first rotates the wheel-speed vector into world coordinates,
$z_w=Rv_{\mathrm{wheel}}$, and then treats that converted observation as
fixed while using $h_w(x)=v$. This gives
\[
 H_{\mathrm{book}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_3&0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Here we instead retain the body-frame measurement model $h(x)=R^Tv$,
which explicitly accounts for both velocity and rotation perturbations.
These are different observation formulations, not an algebraic correction
to the textbook. The body-frame model also makes clear that the two
zero-valued components are modeling constraints rather than encoder
measurements.

\section{Post-Processing of ESKF Error State}

After the prediction and observation-update processes, the Kalman
filter has obtained an estimate of the error state.  The remaining
steps are to inject this estimated error into the nominal state and
then reset the ESKF error state.

\subsection{Error-state injection into the nominal state}

Let the estimated error-state correction be
\[
\delta x_k
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p_k&
\delta v_k&
\delta\theta_k&
\delta b_{g,k}&
\delta b_{a,k}&
\delta g_k
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]
The nominal state is corrected component by component:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p_{k+1} &= p_k+\delta p_k,\\
v_{k+1} &= v_k+\delta v_k,\\
R_{k+1} &= R_k\Exp(\delta\theta_k),\\
b_{g,k+1} &= b_{g,k}+\delta b_{g,k},\\
b_{a,k+1} &= b_{a,k}+\delta b_{a,k},\\
g_{k+1} &= g_k+\delta g_k.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

The rotation correction is applied through the exponential map rather
than ordinary matrix addition.

\subsection{Error-state mean reset}

Once the estimated error has been injected into the nominal state, the
error-state mean is reset:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x^+=0.
}
\end{equation}

The estimated error has not been discarded; it has already been
absorbed into the corrected nominal state.  The reset simply makes the
corrected nominal state the new reference point for the next ESKF
cycle.

\subsection{Covariance reset and change of tangent space}

Before the reset, the covariance $P$ describes the error distribution
in the tangent space associated with the old nominal state.  After the
nominal state is corrected, the error state is defined relative to a
new reference point.  For Euclidean state components this change does
not introduce an additional transformation, but the rotation component
requires special treatment.

Let $R_k$ be the nominal rotation before injection, and let
$\delta\theta_k$ be the estimated rotation correction obtained from the
Kalman update.  After injection,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R^+
=
R_k\Exp(\delta\theta_k).
}
\end{equation}

Let $\delta\theta$ denote a rotation-error random variable expressed
relative to the old nominal rotation, and let $\delta\theta^+$ denote
the same rotation uncertainty expressed relative to the new nominal
rotation.  Both descriptions must represent the same physical
rotation:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R^+\Exp(\delta\theta^+)
=
R_k\Exp(\delta\theta).
}
\end{equation}

Substituting $R^+=R_k\Exp(\delta\theta_k)$ gives
\[
R_k\Exp(\delta\theta_k)\Exp(\delta\theta^+)
=
R_k\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]
Canceling $R_k$ and left-multiplying by
$\Exp(-\delta\theta_k)$ yields
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Exp(\delta\theta^+)
=
\Exp(-\delta\theta_k)\Exp(\delta\theta).
}
\end{equation}

Using the linearized BCH formula for small $\delta\theta$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\theta^+
\approx
-\delta\theta_k
+\delta\theta
-\frac{1}{2}\delta\theta_k^\wedge\delta\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Therefore the reset Jacobian for the rotation-error component is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_\theta
\triangleq
\frac{\partial\delta\theta^+}{\partial\delta\theta}
\approx
I_3-\frac{1}{2}\delta\theta_k^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Extending this transformation to the complete error state gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_k
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
J_\theta,\,
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
I_3
\right).
}
\end{equation}

The covariance must therefore be transformed to the tangent space of
the corrected nominal state:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P_{\mathrm{reset}}
=
J_k P J_k^T.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Interpretation}

The post-processing stage can be summarized as
\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{Kalman update estimates }\delta x_k
\\[1mm]
\downarrow
\\[1mm]
\text{Inject }\delta x_k\text{ into the nominal state}
\\[1mm]
\downarrow
\\[1mm]
\delta x^+\leftarrow 0
\\[1mm]
\downarrow
\\[1mm]
P_{\mathrm{reset}}=J_kPJ_k^T.
\end{array}
}
\]

The covariance-reset operation is a change of tangent-space reference:
the Gaussian error distribution is re-expressed around the corrected
nominal state.  Since $\delta\theta_k$ is normally small,
\[
J_\theta
=
I_3-\frac12\delta\theta_k^\wedge
\approx I_3,
\]
so many ESKF implementations neglect this small correction and use
$P_{\mathrm{reset}}\approx P$.  Nevertheless, the reset Jacobian makes
the tangent-space transformation explicit.

\section{Detailed BCH Derivation for the Rotation Observation Jacobian}

For completeness, this appendix derives the right-BCH linear
approximation used in the update-process section.

Let
\[
\phi=\Log R,
\qquad
R=\Exp(\phi).
\]
Then
\[
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right)
=
\Log\!\left(\Exp(\phi)\Exp(\delta\theta)\right).
\]

The BCH series gives
\[
\log(e^Ae^B)
=
A+B+\frac12[A,B]
+\frac1{12}[A,[A,B]]
+\frac1{12}[B,[B,A]]
+\cdots.
\]
Set
\[
A=\phi^\wedge,
\qquad
B=\delta\theta^\wedge.
\]
Keeping only terms that are first order in $\delta\theta$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log\!\left(
e^{\phi^\wedge}e^{\delta\theta^\wedge}
\right)
&=
\phi^\wedge+\delta\theta^\wedge
+\frac12[\phi^\wedge,\delta\theta^\wedge]\\
&\quad
+\frac1{12}
[\phi^\wedge,[\phi^\wedge,\delta\theta^\wedge]]
+\cdots
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2).
\end{aligned}
\]

For $\SO(3)$,
\[
[a^\wedge,b^\wedge]=(a^\wedge b)^\wedge.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\Log\!\left(
\Exp(\phi)\Exp(\delta\theta)
\right)
=
\phi+
\left[
I+\frac12\phi^\wedge
+\frac1{12}(\phi^\wedge)^2+\cdots
\right]\delta\theta
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2).
\]
The bracketed series is the inverse right Jacobian,
\[
J_r^{-1}(\phi)
=
I+\frac12\phi^\wedge
+\frac1{12}(\phi^\wedge)^2+\cdots.
\]
Hence
\[
\Log\!\left(
\Exp(\phi)\Exp(\delta\theta)
\right)
=
\phi+J_r^{-1}(\phi)\delta\theta
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2),
\]
which gives
\[
\boxed{
\left.
\frac{\partial\Log(R\Exp(\delta\theta))}
{\partial\delta\theta}
\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
=
J_r^{-1}(\Log R).
}
\]
