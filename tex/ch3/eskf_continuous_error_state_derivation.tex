\section{Continuous-Time ESKF Error-State Derivation}

\subsection{Nominal state and IMU motion model}

Define the nominal state
\[
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
p & v & R & b_g & b_a & g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

The continuous-time IMU motion equations are
\begin{align}
\dot p &= v,\\
\dot v &= R(\tilde a-b_a-\eta_a)+g,\\
\dot R &= R(\tilde\omega-b_g-\eta_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_g &= \eta_{bg},\\
\dot b_a &= \eta_{ba},\\
\dot g &= 0.
\end{align}

The IMU measurement equations may be written as
\begin{align}
\tilde a &= R^T(a-g)+b_a+\eta_a,\\
\tilde\omega &= \omega+b_g+\eta_g,
\end{align}
together with
\[
\dot R=R\omega^\wedge.
\]

For the nominal state, the kinematic equations have the same form but
ignore the measurement-noise terms:
\begin{align}
\dot p &= v,\\
\dot v &= R(\tilde a-b_a)+g,\\
\dot R &= R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_g &= 0,\\
\dot b_a &= 0,\\
\dot g &= 0.
\end{align}

\subsection{True state}

Define the true state as
\[
x_t=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_t & v_t & R_t & b_{at} & b_{gt} & g_t
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

Its continuous-time dynamics are
\begin{align}
\dot p_t &= v_t,\\
\dot v_t &= R_t(\tilde a-b_{at}-\eta_a)+g_t,\\
\dot R_t &= R_t(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge,\\
\dot b_{gt} &= \eta_{bg},\\
\dot b_{at} &= \eta_{ba},\\
\dot g_t &= 0.
\end{align}

\subsection{Definition of the error state}

Use a right-multiplicative rotation error:
\begin{align}
p_t &= p+\delta p,\\
v_t &= v+\delta v,\\
R_t &= R\,\delta R
    =R\Exp(\delta\theta),\\
b_{gt} &= b_g+\delta b_g,\\
b_{at} &= b_a+\delta b_a,\\
g_t &= g+\delta g.
\end{align}

The quantities without the subscript $t$ are nominal-state variables.
The goal is to derive the continuous-time dynamics of
\[
\delta x=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p &
\delta v &
\delta\theta &
\delta b_g &
\delta b_a &
\delta g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
\]

\subsection{Position error}

Starting from
\[
p_t=p+\delta p,
\]
differentiate with respect to time:
\[
\dot p_t=\dot p+\delta\dot p.
\]

Since
\[
\dot p_t=v_t=v+\delta v
\]
and
\[
\dot p=v,
\]
we obtain
\[
v+\delta v=v+\delta\dot p.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot p=\delta v.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Velocity error}

Start from
\[
v_t=v+\delta v.
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot v_t=\dot v+\delta\dot v.
\]

Using the true-state equation,
\[
\dot v_t
=
R_t(\tilde a-b_{at}-\eta_a)+g_t.
\]

Substitute the error-state definitions:
\[
\dot v_t
=
R\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde a-b_a-\delta b_a-\eta_a)
+g+\delta g.
\]

For small $\delta\theta$,
\[
\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
I+\delta\theta^\wedge
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2).
\]

Keeping only first-order error terms,
\begin{align}
\dot v_t
&\approx
R(I+\delta\theta^\wedge)
(\tilde a-b_a-\delta b_a-\eta_a)
+g+\delta g\\
&\approx
R\tilde a-Rb_a-R\delta b_a-R\eta_a
+R\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde a
-R\delta\theta^\wedge b_a
+g+\delta g.
\end{align}

Terms such as
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge\delta b_a,
\qquad
\delta\theta^\wedge\eta_a
\]
are neglected as higher-order terms in this first-order error-state
linearization.

Using
\[
a^\wedge b=-b^\wedge a,
\]
we have
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde a
=
-\tilde a^\wedge\delta\theta,
\qquad
-\delta\theta^\wedge b_a
=
b_a^\wedge\delta\theta.
\]

Hence
\[
\dot v_t
\approx
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-R\eta_a
+\delta g.
\]

On the other hand, the nominal equation gives
\[
\dot v+\delta\dot v
=
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g+\delta\dot v.
\]

Comparing the two expressions,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g.
}
\end{equation}

Here we use the standard ESKF noise assumption adopted in the text:
multiplying the zero-mean white accelerometer noise by a rotation does
not change its distribution. Thus, in distribution,
\[
R\eta_a \sim \eta_a,
\]
and the rotated noise term is written simply as
\[
R\eta_a \;\Rightarrow\; \eta_a.
\]
This is a distributional simplification rather than a pointwise identity
between individual noise samples.

\subsection{Rotation error}

Start from
\[
R_t=R\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot R_t
=
\dot R\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta).
\]

\subsubsection*{Exact derivative of the exponential map}

For a general time-varying
\[
\delta\theta(t)\in\R^3,
\]
the exact derivative is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge,
}
\end{equation}
where $J_r(\delta\theta)$ is the right Jacobian of $\SO(3)$.

The formula above can be seen directly from the limit definition of the
right Jacobian.

Define
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

The right/local tangent velocity of $Q(t)$ is
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left(
Q(t)^{-1}Q(t+h)
\right)
}{h}.
\]

Since
\[
Q(t)^{-1}
=
\Exp(-\delta\theta(t))
\]
and
\[
Q(t+h)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+h)),
\]
we have
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\delta\theta(t))
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+h))
\right]
}{h}.
\]

Using the ordinary first-order expansion
\[
\delta\theta(t+h)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
h\,\delta\dot\theta(t)
+
O(h^2),
\]
this becomes
\[
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
\delta\theta+h\,\delta\dot\theta
\right)
\right]
}{h}.
\]

Now compare this with the directional-limit definition of the right
Jacobian:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)u
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
\Log\!\left[
\Exp(-\phi)
\Exp(\phi+hu)
\right]
}{h}.
}
\end{equation}

By identifying
\[
\phi=\delta\theta,
\qquad
u=\delta\dot\theta,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_{\mathrm{local}}
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Since a right/local tangent velocity satisfies
\[
\dot Q
=
Q\,\omega_{\mathrm{local}}^\wedge,
\]
we get
\[
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\]

Substituting
\[
Q=\Exp(\delta\theta)
\]
gives the exact relation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, this is the same relation as the compact right-Jacobian
increment formula
\[
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

To see why these two forms are equivalent, define
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

Starting from the differential form,
\[
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge,
\]
the ordinary first-order Taylor expansion in time gives
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)+\dot Q(t)\,dt+O(dt^2).
\]

Substituting the differential equation,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Factoring out $Q(t)$,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
\left[
I+
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
\right]
+
O(dt^2).
\]

For a small vector $\epsilon$,
\[
\Exp(\epsilon)
=
I+\epsilon^\wedge+O(\|\epsilon\|^2).
\]

Choose
\[
\epsilon
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt.
\]

Then
\[
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
=
I+
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Therefore
\[
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

Since
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t))
\]
and
\[
\delta\theta(t+dt)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
\delta\dot\theta(t)\,dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Conversely, start from the increment form:
\[
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
\]

Using the small-angle expansion of the second exponential,
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2).
\]

Hence
\[
\frac{Q(t+dt)-Q(t)}{dt}
=
Q(t)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge
+
O(dt).
\]

Taking the limit $dt\to0$ gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Thus the increment form and the differential form describe the same
local motion:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
\\[4mm]
\Updownarrow\; dt\to0
\\[4mm]
\dfrac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

The upper expression is a first-order finite-increment relation, while
the lower expression is the corresponding exact instantaneous
differential relation.

Since
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)
=
I-\frac12\delta\theta^\wedge
+O(\|\delta\theta\|^2),
\]
the first-order ESKF approximation is
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\approx
\delta\dot\theta.
\]

Therefore, within the first-order error-state derivation,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Using this first-order approximation,
\[
\dot R_t
\approx
\dot R\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
\]

The nominal rotation dynamics are
\[
\dot R
=
R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge.
\]

Hence
\[
\dot R_t
\approx
R(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Exp(\delta\theta)
+
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge.
\]

The true rotation dynamics are
\[
\dot R_t
=
R_t(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge
=
R\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge.
\]

Equating both expressions and canceling $R$ gives
\begin{align}
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge
&=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge\\
&\quad
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge
\Exp(\delta\theta).
\end{align}

Use the $\SO(3)$ identity
\[
\phi^\wedge R
=
R(R^T\phi)^\wedge.
\]

With
\[
R=\Exp(\delta\theta),
\]
this gives
\[
(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)
\right)^\wedge.
\]

Therefore
\begin{align}
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge
&=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\Big[
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)^\wedge\\
&\qquad
-\left(
\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)
\right)^\wedge
\Big].
\end{align}

For small $\delta\theta$,
\[
\Exp(-\delta\theta)
\approx
I-\delta\theta^\wedge.
\]

Thus
\begin{align}
&(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)
-\Exp(-\delta\theta)(\tilde\omega-b_g)\\
&\approx
(\tilde\omega-b_{gt}-\eta_g)
-(I-\delta\theta^\wedge)(\tilde\omega-b_g)\\
&=
b_g-b_{gt}-\eta_g
+\delta\theta^\wedge\tilde\omega
-\delta\theta^\wedge b_g.
\end{align}

Since
\[
b_{gt}=b_g+\delta b_g,
\]
we have
\[
b_g-b_{gt}=-\delta b_g.
\]

Also,
\[
\delta\theta^\wedge(\tilde\omega-b_g)
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta.
\]

Therefore
\[
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g.
\]

Hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Identity used above}

For $R\in\SO(3)$,
\[
\operatorname{Ad}_R\phi
=
(R\phi^\wedge R^T)^\vee
=
R\phi.
\]

Thus
\[
R\phi^\wedge R^T=(R\phi)^\wedge.
\]

From this,
\[
\phi^\wedge R
=
R(R^T\phi)^\wedge.
\]

\subsection{Supplement: From Finite Infinitesimal Increments to Instantaneous Velocity}

This appendix clarifies how the right-Jacobian finite-increment relation
is used to obtain the instantaneous right/local tangent velocity.

Let
\[
Q(t)=\Exp(\delta\theta(t)).
\]

For a small time increment $dt$,
\[
\delta\theta(t+dt)
=
\delta\theta(t)
+
\delta\dot\theta(t)\,dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
and therefore
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta(t+dt))
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta+\delta\dot\theta\,dt).
\]

The right-Jacobian increment relation is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Exp(\phi+\Delta\phi)
\approx
\Exp(\phi)
\Exp\!\left(J_r(\phi)\Delta\phi\right).
}
\end{equation}

Set
\[
\phi=\delta\theta,
\qquad
\Delta\phi=\delta\dot\theta\,dt.
\]

Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)
\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Now define the instantaneous right/local tangent velocity
$\omega_R$ by the infinitesimal motion
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)\Exp(\omega_R\,dt).
}
\end{equation}

Comparing the two infinitesimal expressions gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_R
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

Thus $\delta\dot\theta$ is the time derivative of the exponential
coordinates, whereas $J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta$ is the
corresponding right/local tangent velocity of the rotation matrix.

To recover the ordinary matrix derivative, use
\[
\Exp(\omega_R\,dt)
=
I+\omega_R^\wedge dt+O(dt^2).
\]

Hence
\[
Q(t+dt)
=
Q(t)
+
Q(t)\omega_R^\wedge dt
+
O(dt^2),
\]
so
\[
\frac{Q(t+dt)-Q(t)}{dt}
=
Q(t)\omega_R^\wedge
+
O(dt).
\]

Taking $dt\to0$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot Q
=
Q\omega_R^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Substituting
\[
\omega_R
=
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\]
and
\[
Q=\Exp(\delta\theta)
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
=
\Exp(\delta\theta)
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Therefore the finite infinitesimal increment and the instantaneous
differential form are two descriptions of the same local motion:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
Q(t+dt)
\approx
Q(t)\Exp\!\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta\,dt
\right)
\\[4mm]
\Downarrow\quad dt\to0
\\[4mm]
\dot Q
=
Q
\left(
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\right)^\wedge.
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Exact and small-angle forms of the right Jacobian}

For $\phi\in\R^3$, let
\[
\vartheta=\|\phi\|.
\]

The exact right Jacobian of $\SO(3)$ is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)
=
I
-
\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\vartheta^2}\phi^\wedge
+
\frac{\vartheta-\sin\vartheta}{\vartheta^3}
(\phi^\wedge)^2.
}
\end{equation}

The coefficient of the first-order term follows from
\[
\cos\vartheta
=
1-\frac{\vartheta^2}{2!}
+\frac{\vartheta^4}{4!}
-\cdots,
\]
so
\[
\frac{1-\cos\vartheta}{\vartheta^2}
=
\frac12-\frac{\vartheta^2}{24}+\cdots.
\]

Similarly,
\[
\sin\vartheta
=
\vartheta-\frac{\vartheta^3}{3!}
+\frac{\vartheta^5}{5!}
-\cdots,
\]
which gives
\[
\frac{\vartheta-\sin\vartheta}{\vartheta^3}
=
\frac16-\frac{\vartheta^2}{120}+\cdots.
\]

Therefore
\[
J_r(\phi)
=
I
-\frac12\phi^\wedge
+\frac16(\phi^\wedge)^2
+O(\|\phi\|^3),
\]
and, retaining only first-order terms,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\phi)
=
I-\frac12\phi^\wedge+O(\|\phi\|^2).
}
\end{equation}

For the ESKF rotation error, $\phi=\delta\theta$ is small. Hence
\[
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
=
\delta\dot\theta
-\frac12
\delta\theta^\wedge\delta\dot\theta
+\cdots.
\]

Under the first-order error-state approximation, the product involving
$\delta\theta$ and $\delta\dot\theta$ is neglected, yielding
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
J_r(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta
\approx
\delta\dot\theta.
}
\end{equation}

This is why the ESKF derivation commonly uses
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\Exp(\delta\theta)
\approx
\Exp(\delta\theta)\delta\dot\theta^\wedge,
\]
even though the exact differential relation contains the right
Jacobian.

\subsection{Gyroscope-bias error}

Starting from
\[
b_{gt}=b_g+\delta b_g,
\]
differentiate:
\[
\dot b_{gt}
=
\dot b_g+\delta\dot b_g.
\]

The true bias dynamics are
\[
\dot b_{gt}=\eta_{bg},
\]
while the nominal model ignores noise:
\[
\dot b_g=0.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Accelerometer-bias error}

Similarly,
\[
b_{at}=b_a+\delta b_a.
\]

Differentiating,
\[
\dot b_{at}
=
\dot b_a+\delta\dot b_a.
\]

Using
\[
\dot b_{at}=\eta_{ba},
\qquad
\dot b_a=0,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gravity error}

Starting from
\[
g_t=g+\delta g,
\]
differentiate:
\[
\dot g_t=\dot g+\delta\dot g.
\]

Since
\[
\dot g_t=0,
\qquad
\dot g=0,
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta\dot g=0.
}
\end{equation}

\section{Final continuous-time error-state equations}

Collecting the results,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta\dot p
&=
\delta v,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot v
&=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot\theta
&=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g,\\[1mm]
\delta\dot b_g
&=
\eta_{bg},\\[1mm]
\delta\dot b_a
&=
\eta_{ba},\\[1mm]
\delta\dot g
&=
0.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}



\section{Discrete-Time ESKF Kinematic Equation}

\subsection*{Notation convention}

In the continuous-time derivations below, $t$, $\tau$, and $s$ denote
physical time. After discretization, the final propagation equations
use the discrete time-step indices $k$ and $k+1$, with
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
t_k=t,\qquad t_{k+1}=t_k+\Delta t=t+\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

Thus, continuous-time integration uses $t,\tau,s$, while final
discrete-time propagation equations use $k,k+1$.


\subsection{Nominal-state position equation}

For the nominal state,
\[
\dot p=v,
\qquad
\dot v=R(\tilde a-b_a)+g.
\]

Define the acceleration expressed in the world frame as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
a_W(t)=R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g.
}
\end{equation}

Since
\[
\dot p(\tau)=v(\tau),
\]
integrating from $t$ to $t+\Delta t$ gives
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+\int_t^{t+\Delta t}v(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

The velocity at an intermediate time $\tau$ is
\[
v(\tau)
=
v(t)+\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds.
\]

Substituting this into the position integral,
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\left[
v(t)+\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds
\right]d\tau.
\]

\subsubsection*{Assumption over one IMU sampling interval}

To obtain the discrete-time equation used here, assume that the
world-frame acceleration is approximately constant over the short IMU
sampling interval:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
a_W(s)\approx a_W(t),
\qquad
s\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, over this interval we use the values at the beginning of
the interval,
\[
R(s)\approx R(t),
\qquad
\tilde a(s)\approx\tilde a(t),
\qquad
b_a(s)\approx b_a(t),
\]
with gravity $g$ treated as constant. Thus
\[
a_W(s)
\approx
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g.
\]

Under this approximation,
\[
\int_t^\tau a_W(s)\,ds
\approx
a_W(t)(\tau-t).
\]

Therefore
\begin{align}
p(t+\Delta t)
&=
p(t)+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\left[
v(t)+a_W(t)(\tau-t)
\right]d\tau\\
&=
p(t)
+
v(t)\Delta t
+
a_W(t)
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}(\tau-t)\,d\tau.
\end{align}

The remaining integral is
\[
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}(\tau-t)\,d\tau
=
\frac12\Delta t^2.
\]

Hence
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+v(t)\Delta t
+\frac12a_W(t)\Delta t^2.
\]

Substituting the expression for $a_W(t)$,
\[
p(t+\Delta t)
=
p(t)+v(t)\Delta t
+
\frac12
\left[
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g
\right]
\Delta t^2.
\]

Finally,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
p_{k+1}
=
p_k+v_k\Delta t
+
\frac12R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})\Delta t^2
+
\frac12g_k\Delta t^2.
}
\end{equation}

This is the discrete-time nominal position propagation equation.  The
appearance of $R(t)$ rather than $R(t+\Delta t)$ follows from the
beginning-of-interval approximation above.  In the exact continuous
motion, $R(s)$ and therefore $a_W(s)$ generally vary throughout the
sampling interval.

\subsection{Nominal-state velocity equation}

Starting from
\[
\dot v
=
R(\tilde a-b_a)+g
\equiv
a_W,
\]
integrate over the interval $[t,t+\Delta t]$:
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
=
v(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
a_W(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

Using the same assumption as in the position derivation,
\[
a_W(\tau)\approx a_W(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t],
\]
we obtain
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
\approx
v(t)
+
a_W(t)
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}d\tau.
\]

Therefore
\[
v(t+\Delta t)
=
v(t)
+
a_W(t)\Delta t.
\]

Substituting
\[
a_W(t)
=
R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))+g,
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
v_{k+1}
=
v_k
+
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})\Delta t
+
g_k\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

This is the discrete-time nominal velocity propagation equation.  As
with the position equation, the approximation uses the beginning-of-
interval values $R(t)$, $\tilde a(t)$, and $b_a(t)$ over one IMU
sampling interval.


\subsection{Nominal-state rotation equation}

The continuous-time nominal rotation dynamics are
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\dot R(t)
=
R(t)(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))^\wedge.
}
\end{equation}

Define the bias-corrected body-frame angular velocity
\[
\omega_B(t)
\equiv
\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t).
\]

Over one short IMU sampling interval, assume
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\omega_B(\tau)\approx\omega_B(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Equivalently,
\[
\tilde\omega(\tau)\approx\tilde\omega(t),
\qquad
b_g(\tau)\approx b_g(t)
\]
over this interval.  The differential equation therefore becomes
\[
\dot R(\tau)
=
R(\tau)\omega_B(t)^\wedge,
\]
where $\omega_B(t)^\wedge$ is constant with respect to $\tau$.

The solution of this matrix differential equation with initial
condition $R(t)$ is
\[
R(\tau)
=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right),
\]
where $\exp(\cdot)$ denotes the matrix exponential.

This can be verified directly:
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{d\tau}R(\tau)
&=
R(t)
\frac{d}{d\tau}
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right)
\\
&=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge(\tau-t)
\right)
\omega_B(t)^\wedge
\\
&=
R(\tau)\omega_B(t)^\wedge.
\end{align}

At $\tau=t+\Delta t$,
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
R(t)
\exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)^\wedge\Delta t
\right).
\]

Since
\[
\Exp(\phi)
\equiv
\exp(\phi^\wedge)
\]
and
\[
(\omega_B\Delta t)^\wedge
=
\omega_B^\wedge\Delta t,
\]
we obtain
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
R(t)
\Exp\!\left(
\omega_B(t)\Delta t
\right).
\]

Substituting
\[
\omega_B(t)
=
\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t),
\]
gives the discrete-time nominal rotation propagation equation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
R_{k+1}
=
R_k
\Exp\!\left(
(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right).
}
\end{equation}

The exponential increment appears on the right because
\[
\dot R=R\omega_B^\wedge
\]
uses the body-frame (right-trivialized) angular velocity.  In contrast,
if the angular velocity were expressed in the world frame so that
\[
\dot R=\omega_W^\wedge R,
\]
the corresponding constant-angular-velocity propagation would be
\[
R(t+\Delta t)
=
\Exp(\omega_W\Delta t)R(t).
\]



\subsection{Nominal-state bias and gravity equations}

For the nominal state, the gyroscope bias, accelerometer bias, and
gravity are modeled as constant during propagation:
\[
\dot b_g=0,
\qquad
\dot b_a=0,
\qquad
\dot g=0.
\]

Integrating the gyroscope-bias equation over one sampling interval,
\[
b_g(t+\Delta t)
=
b_g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot b_g(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
b_{g,k+1}=b_{g,k}.
}
\end{equation}

Similarly,
\[
b_a(t+\Delta t)
=
b_a(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot b_a(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
so
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
b_{a,k+1}=b_{a,k}.
}
\end{equation}

For gravity,
\[
g(t+\Delta t)
=
g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\dot g(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
g_{k+1}=g_k.
}
\end{equation}

The constant nominal-bias equations do not imply that the physical IMU
biases are assumed to remain exactly constant.  Their random-walk
variations are represented in the error-state dynamics by
\[
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg},
\qquad
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba},
\]
and their uncertainty is propagated through the error-state covariance.


\subsection{Summary of the discrete-time nominal-state equations}

Collecting the nominal-state propagation equations,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p_{k+1}
&=
p_k+v_k\Delta t
+\frac12
\left[
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})+g_k
\right]\Delta t^2,
\\[1mm]
v_{k+1}
&=
v_k+
\left[
R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})+g_k
\right]\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
R_{k+1}
&=
R_k
\Exp\!\left(
(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right),
\\[1mm]
b_{g,k+1}
&=
b_{g,k},
\\
b_{a,k+1}
&=
b_{a,k},
\\
g_{k+1}
&=
g_k.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-time error-state equations}

We now discretize the continuous-time error-state equations derived
previously.

\subsubsection*{Position error}

From the continuous-time position-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot p=\delta v,
\]
assume that $\delta v$ is approximately constant over one short
sampling interval $[t,t+\Delta t]$. Then
\begin{align}
\delta p(t+\Delta t)-\delta p(t)
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta\dot p(\tau)\,d\tau\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta v(\tau)\,d\tau\\
&\approx
\delta v(t)\Delta t.
\end{align}

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta p_{k+1}
=
\delta p_k+\delta v_k\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Velocity error}

From the continuous-time velocity-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
-\eta_a
+\delta g,
\]
define the deterministic part
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
f_v
=
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\delta\theta
-R\delta b_a
+\delta g.
}
\end{equation}

Then
\[
\delta\dot v=f_v-\eta_a.
\]

Integrating over one sampling interval,
\begin{align}
\delta v(t+\Delta t)-\delta v(t)
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f_v(\tau)\,d\tau
-
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_a(\tau)\,d\tau.
\end{align}

Assume that the deterministic quantities in $f_v$ are approximately
constant over the short interval:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
f_v(\tau)\approx f_v(t),
\qquad
\tau\in[t,t+\Delta t].
}
\end{equation}

Thus
\[
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}f_v(\tau)\,d\tau
\approx
f_v(t)\Delta t.
\]

Define the integrated accelerometer-noise increment
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_v
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_a(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

Assume the continuous-time accelerometer noise is zero-mean white
Gaussian noise with
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_a(t)]=0
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_a(t)\eta_a(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_a^2 I\,\delta(t-s).
}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\sigma_a^2$ is the continuous-time white-noise intensity
(per axis).  Then the integrated noise increment also has zero mean:
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_v]=0.
\]

Its covariance is
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_v)
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_v\eta_v^T
\right]
\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_a(\tau)\eta_a(s)^T
\right]
\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\sigma_a^2 I
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\delta(\tau-s)\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\boxed{
\sigma_a^2\Delta t\,I.
}
\end{align}

Thus, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_v)
=
\sigma_a^2\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

The book distinguishes the sampled accelerometer measurement noise from
the continuous-time noise-density parameter through
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma_a(k)
=
\frac{\sigma_a}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
}
\end{equation}

Therefore the standard deviation of the integrated velocity-noise
increment can be written in either equivalent form:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma(\eta_v)
=
\Delta t\,\sigma_a(k)
=
\sigma_a\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

The first expression uses the sampled-noise notation, while the
second follows directly after substituting the sampled-noise relation.

Note that writing simply
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_a)=\sigma_a^2
\]
for continuous-time white noise is shorthand for its noise intensity.
The Dirac-delta covariance above makes the continuous-to-discrete
scaling explicit.

Hence
\[
\delta v(t+\Delta t)
\approx
\delta v(t)+f_v(t)\Delta t-\eta_v.
\]

Substituting the expression for $f_v(t)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta v(t+\Delta t)
&=
\delta v(t)
-R(t)(\tilde a(t)-b_a(t))^\wedge
 \delta\theta(t)\Delta t\\
&\quad
-R(t)\delta b_a(t)\Delta t
+\delta g(t)\Delta t
-\eta_v.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

The term $\eta_v$ is the discrete-time noise increment obtained by
integrating the continuous-time accelerometer noise over the sampling
interval; unlike the deterministic term $f_v(t)\Delta t$, it should not
be interpreted simply as a deterministic constant multiplied by
$\Delta t$.


\subsubsection*{Rotation error}

From the continuous-time rotation-error equation,
\[
\delta\dot\theta
=
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\delta\theta
-\delta b_g
-\eta_g,
\]
define
\[
A
\equiv
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge.
\]

Over one short IMU sampling interval, assume that
\[
\tilde\omega(\tau)\approx\tilde\omega(t),
\qquad
b_g(\tau)\approx b_g(t),
\]
so that $A$ is approximately constant.  The homogeneous equation
\[
\dot x=Ax
\]
has the solution
\[
x(t+\Delta t)=\exp(A\Delta t)x(t).
\]

Therefore the homogeneous part of the rotation error propagates as
\[
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
=
\exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))^\wedge\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta(t).
\]

Using the $\SO(3)$ exponential-map notation,
\[
\exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge\Delta t
\right)
=
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega-b_g)\Delta t
\right).
\]

For the forcing terms $-\delta b_g-\eta_g$, the first-order
discretization used here gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
&\approx
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega(t)-b_g(t))\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta(t)
\\
&\quad
-\delta b_g(t)\Delta t
-\eta_\theta,
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}
where the integrated gyroscope-noise increment is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_\theta
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_g(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

More exactly, for constant
\[
A=-(\tilde\omega-b_g)^\wedge,
\]
the variation-of-constants formula is
\[
\delta\theta(t+\Delta t)
=
e^{A\Delta t}\delta\theta(t)
-
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
e^{A(t+\Delta t-\tau)}
\left[
\delta b_g(\tau)+\eta_g(\tau)
\right]d\tau.
\]
The expression above with
$-\delta b_g\Delta t-\eta_\theta$ is the corresponding first-order
forcing-term approximation.

\paragraph{Variance of the discrete rotation-noise increment.}

Assume the continuous-time gyroscope noise is zero-mean isotropic white
Gaussian noise with
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_g(t)]=0
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_g(t)\eta_g(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_g^2 I\,\delta(t-s).
}
\end{equation}

Then
\[
\mathbb{E}[\eta_\theta]=0
\]
and
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_\theta)
&=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_\theta\eta_\theta^T
\right]
\\
&=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_g(\tau)\eta_g(s)^T
\right]
\,d\tau\,ds
\\
&=
\boxed{
\sigma_g^2\Delta t\,I.
}
\end{align}

Thus, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_\theta)
=
\sigma_g^2\Delta t.
}
\end{equation}

For the sampled gyroscope measurement noise, the book uses
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma_g(k)
=
\frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
}
\end{equation}

Hence the standard deviation of the integrated rotation-noise increment
can be written as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\sigma(\eta_\theta)
=
\Delta t\,\sigma_g(k)
=
\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

Again, the first form uses the sampled-noise notation, while the second is the
equivalent expression in terms of the continuous-time noise-density
parameter.  The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ factor belongs to the standard
deviation, not to the variance.  The same covariance result also holds if the
exact state-transition rotation inside the noise integral is retained:
because $A$ is skew-symmetric, $e^{As}$ is a rotation matrix, and for
isotropic noise
\[
e^{As}(\sigma_g^2 I)e^{As\,T}
=
\sigma_g^2 I.
\]

\subsubsection*{Gyroscope-bias error}

Here we explicitly distinguish the continuous-time bias random-walk
process from its integrated discrete-time increment.  The continuous
process is denoted by
\[
\eta_{bg}(t),
\]
while
\[
\eta_{bg,d}
\]
denotes the noise accumulated over one sampling interval.  The book
reuses the symbol $\eta_{bg}$ for the discrete increment in
Eqs.~(3.42)--(3.43); the subscript $d$ is retained here to avoid
ambiguity.

From
\[
\delta\dot b_g=\eta_{bg},
\]
integrating over one sampling interval gives
\[
\delta b_g(t+\Delta t)
=
\delta b_g(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{bg}(\tau)\,d\tau.
\]

Define
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_{bg,d}
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{bg}(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta b_{g,k+1}
=
\delta b_{g,k}+\eta_{bg,d}.
}
\end{equation}

If
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\eta_{bg}(t)\eta_{bg}(s)^T
\right]
=
\sigma_{bg}^2 I\,\delta(t-s),
\]
then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{bg,d})
=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t\,I,
}
\end{equation}
or, per axis,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{bg,d})
=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t,
\qquad
\sigma_{\eta_{bg,d}}
=
\sigma_{bg}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Accelerometer-bias error}

Likewise, $\eta_{ba}(t)$ denotes the continuous-time accelerometer-bias
random-walk process, while $\eta_{ba,d}$ denotes its integrated
discrete-time increment over one sampling interval.

Similarly, from
\[
\delta\dot b_a=\eta_{ba},
\]
we obtain
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta b_a(t+\Delta t)
=
\delta b_a(t)+\eta_{ba,d},
}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\eta_{ba,d}
\equiv
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\eta_{ba}(\tau)\,d\tau.
}
\end{equation}

For continuous-time isotropic white noise intensity
$\sigma_{ba}^2$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{ba,d})
=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t\,I,
}
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{ba,d})
=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t,
\qquad
\sigma_{\eta_{ba,d}}
=
\sigma_{ba}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Gravity error}

From
\[
\delta\dot g=0,
\]
integration gives
\[
\delta g(t+\Delta t)-\delta g(t)
=
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\delta\dot g(\tau)\,d\tau
=
0.
\]

Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta g_{k+1}
=
\delta g_k.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Summary of the discrete-time error-state equations}

Collecting the discrete-time error-state propagation equations,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\delta p_{k+1}
&=
\delta p_k+\delta v_k\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\delta v_{k+1}
&=
\delta v_k
-R_k(\tilde a_k-b_{a,k})^\wedge\delta\theta_k\Delta t
-R_k\delta b_{a,k}\Delta t
+\delta g_k\Delta t
-\eta_v,
\\[1mm]
\delta\theta_{k+1}
&\approx
\Exp\!\left(
-(\tilde\omega_k-b_{g,k})\Delta t
\right)\delta\theta_k
-\delta b_{g,k}\Delta t
-\eta_\theta,
\\[1mm]
\delta b_{g,k+1}
&=
\delta b_{g,k}+\eta_{bg,d},
\\
\delta b_{a,k+1}
&=
\delta b_{a,k}+\eta_{ba,d},
\\
\delta g_{k+1}
&=
\delta g_k.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

It is useful to list both the standard deviations and variances.  The sampled IMU measurement-noise
standard deviations satisfy
\[
\sigma_a(k)=\frac{\sigma_a}{\sqrt{\Delta t}},
\qquad
\sigma_g(k)=\frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.
\]

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\eta_v)
&=
\Delta t\,\sigma_a(k)
=
\sigma_a\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_v)
&=
\sigma_a^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_\theta)
&=
\Delta t\,\sigma_g(k)
=
\sigma_g\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_\theta)
&=
\sigma_g^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_{bg,d})
&=
\sigma_{bg}\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{bg,d})
&=
\sigma_{bg}^2\Delta t,
\\[1mm]
\sigma(\eta_{ba,d})
&=
\sigma_{ba}\sqrt{\Delta t},
&
\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{ba,d})
&=
\sigma_{ba}^2\Delta t.
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

The first two standard-deviation expressions use the sampled IMU-noise
notation.  For the bias random walks, the book denotes the
integrated discrete noises again by $\eta_{bg}$ and $\eta_{ba}$; this
note uses $\eta_{bg,d}$ and $\eta_{ba,d}$ so that they remain distinct
from the continuous-time processes $\eta_{bg}(t)$ and $\eta_{ba}(t)$.


\section{Motion Process of ESKF}

Using the discrete-time error-state equations derived above, write the
error-state motion model as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x_{k+1}
=
f(\delta x_k)+w_k,
\qquad
w_k\sim\mathcal N(0,Q_k).
}
\end{equation}

The discrete process-noise covariance is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
Q_k
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
0_3,\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_v),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_\theta),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{bg,d}),\,
\operatorname{Cov}(\eta_{ba,d}),\,
0_3
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Here the subscript $d$ is retained for the bias-noise increments in
order to distinguish them from the corresponding continuous-time
white-noise processes.

\subsection{Linearized form}

Linearize the discrete transition function $f$ about the nominal
error-state mean.  For a general linearization point
$\bar{\delta x}_k$,
\[
f(\delta x_k)
\approx
f(\bar{\delta x}_k)
+
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=\bar{\delta x}_k}
(\delta x_k-\bar{\delta x}_k).
\]

In the ESKF, the error-state mean is reset to zero, so the
linearization point is
\[
\bar{\delta x}_k=0.
\]

Therefore,
\[
f(\delta x_k)
\approx
f(0)
+
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=0}
\delta x_k.
\]

For the error-state motion model,
\[
f(0)=0.
\]

Define the discrete-time error-state transition Jacobian as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
F_k
\triangleq
\left.
\frac{\partial f(\delta x)}
{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{\delta x=0}.
}
\end{equation}

The linearized error-state propagation is therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x_{k+1}
\approx
F_k\delta x_k+w_k.
}
\end{equation}

Thus, $F_k$ is the Jacobian of the \emph{discrete-time error-state
transition function} with respect to the error state, evaluated at
zero error.  It should not be confused with the time derivative
$d\delta x/dt$.

\subsection{State transition Jacobian matrix}

The error-state ordering is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\delta p &
\delta v &
\delta\theta &
\delta b_g &
\delta b_a &
\delta g
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
}
\end{equation}

Since the discrete-time error-state equations have already been
linearized explicitly, the transition Jacobian can be read directly
from the coefficients multiplying each error-state component.  With
the ordering above,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
F_k
=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_3
&
I_3\Delta t
&
0
&
0
&
0
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&
I_3
&
-R(\tilde a-b_a)^\wedge\Delta t
&
0
&
-R\Delta t
&
I_3\Delta t
\\[1mm]
0
&
0
&
\Exp\!\left(-(\tilde\omega-b_g)\Delta t\right)
&
-I_3\Delta t
&
0
&
0
\\[1mm]
0
&

&
0
&
I_3
&

&

\\[1mm]

&

&

&

\\[1mm]

\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

Each entry is a $3\times3$ block, so $F_k\in\R^{18\times18}$.

\subsection{ESKF IMU propagation flow}

For each new IMU measurement, the ESKF prediction process can be
summarized as follows:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{New IMU measurement}
\\
(\tilde a_k,\ \tilde\omega_k,\ \Delta t)
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Nominal-state prediction}
\\
\text{using the discrete-time nominal-state equations}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
[p,\ v,\ R,\ b_g,\ b_a,\ g]^T
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Build the error-state transition matrix }F_k
\\
\text{using the error-state transition Jacobian}
\\[2mm]
\downarrow
\\[-1mm]
\begin{array}{ccc}
\delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_k\delta x_k
&
\qquad
&
P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_kP_kF_k^T+Q_k
\\
\text{usually remains zero}
&&
\text{propagate uncertainty}
\end{array}
\\[3mm]
\downarrow
\\[2mm]
\text{Predicted ESKF state}
\\
\left(
x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}},
\ \delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}},
\ P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
\right).
\end{array}
}
\end{equation}

Mathematically, the prediction contains three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item propagate the nominal state using the IMU measurements;
    \item propagate the error-state mean,
    \[
    \delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}=F_k\delta x_k;
    \]
    \item propagate the error-state covariance,
    \[
    P_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
    =
    F_kP_kF_k^T+Q_k.
    \]
\end{enumerate}

In the ESKF, the error-state mean is reset to zero after the update
step.  Therefore, in the usual prediction cycle,
\[
\delta x_k=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta x_{k+1}^{\mathrm{pred}}
=
F_k\delta x_k
=
0.
\]
Consequently, the explicit error-state mean propagation is often
unnecessary in an implementation.  The important quantities propagated
during the IMU prediction are the nominal state and the error-state
covariance.


\section{Update Process of ESKF}

\subsection{Observation model}

Consider the general observation equation
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
z=h(x)+v,
\qquad
v\sim\mathcal N(0,V).
}
\end{equation}

Here $z$ is the observation data, $v$ is the observation noise, and
$V$ is the observation-noise covariance matrix.

In a traditional EKF, the observation equation is linearized with
respect to the state variables. In the ESKF, the Kalman-filter update
is performed with respect to the error-state variables. Therefore, the
required observation Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H
=
\left.
\frac{\partial h}{\partial\delta x}
\right|_{x_{\mathrm{pred}}}.
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kalman update equations}

Once the observation Jacobian is available, the Kalman gain is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
K
=
P_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T
\left(
HP_{\mathrm{pred}}H^T+V
\right)^{-1}.
}
\end{equation}

The error-state update is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\delta x
=
K\left(
z-h(x_{\mathrm{pred}})
\right).
}
\end{equation}

Following the notation used in this chapter, the nominal state is then
updated as
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
x=x_{\mathrm{pred}}+\delta x.
}
\end{equation}

The covariance update is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P=(I-KH)P_{\mathrm{pred}}.
}
\end{equation}

Here $K$ is the Kalman gain, $P_{\mathrm{pred}}$ is the predicted
error-state covariance, and $P$ is the updated covariance.

\subsection{Observation Jacobian with respect to the error state}

For observations of the nominal state, the Jacobian with respect to the
error state can be obtained using the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
H
=
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}.
}
\end{equation}

The chain of mappings is
\[
\boxed{
\delta x
\longrightarrow
x(\delta x)
\longrightarrow
h\!\left(x(\delta x)\right).
}
\]

Here $x(\delta x)$ denotes a local perturbation of the nominal state
used for linearization. It should not be confused with the true state.

\subsection{Local perturbation parameterization}

For the nominal-state ordering
\[
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
p&
v&
R&
b_g&
b_a&
g
\end{bmatrix}^{T},
\]
define the locally perturbed state by
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
x(\delta x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p+\delta p\\
v+\delta v\\
R\Exp(\delta\theta)\\
b_g+\delta b_g\\
b_a+\delta b_a\\
g+\delta g
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
x(0)=x_{\mathrm{pred}}.
}
\end{equation}

For the Euclidean components,
\[
\frac{\partial(p+\delta p)}{\partial\delta p}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(v+\delta v)}{\partial\delta v}=I_3,
\]
and similarly
\[
\frac{\partial(b_g+\delta b_g)}{\partial\delta b_g}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(b_a+\delta b_a)}{\partial\delta b_a}=I_3,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(g+\delta g)}{\partial\delta g}=I_3.
\]

The rotation component requires special treatment. Since the
perturbation is applied on the right,
\[
R(\delta\theta)=R\Exp(\delta\theta),
\]
and the corresponding rotation coordinate is
\[
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right).
\]

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\left.
\frac{
\partial
\Log\!\left(R\Exp(\delta\theta)\right)
}{
\partial\delta\theta
}
\right|_{\delta\theta=0}
=
J_r^{-1}(\Log R).
\end{equation}

Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\delta x}
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
J_r^{-1}(\Log R),\,
I_3,\,
I_3,\,
I_3
\right).
}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Full-pose GNSS observation}

For the full-pose GNSS case, the observation contains position and
rotation. With the nominal-state ordering above, the observation
function is conceptually
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
h(x)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p\\
R
\end{bmatrix}.
}
\end{equation}

Thus the GNSS observation directly depends on the nominal position
$p$ and nominal rotation $R$, while it does not directly observe
$v$, $b_g$, $b_a$, or $g$.

The next step is to evaluate
\[
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial p}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial v}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial R}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial b_g}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial b_a}
&
\dfrac{\partial h}{\partial g}
\end{bmatrix},
\]
and then combine it with
$\partial x/\partial\delta x$. The treatment of the rotation
observation and its relation to the direct error-state GNSS
formulation is left for the following discussion.
